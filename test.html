What this is testing, and how it differs from Patriot's routes

Patriot

Splits a route into numbered legs ("4 · Fairfield Road · walk NW → · both sides"), with an arrowed, numbered line per leg on the map. Every leg assumes you do both sides in one pass, so you either zig-zag across the road the whole way or walk each close in and back out twice.

This version

Treats each side of a road as its own pavement and walks each one once. The rule is simple: keep the road on your left, and the letterboxes you're doing are on your right. Follow it round every corner and you go up one side of a close, cross at the end, and come back down the other. When you come back out onto the main road, you carry on along the side you were on.

It's the road on your left so that in the UK the nearest traffic is coming towards you. You can see lorries, puddle splashes and gusts coming, the same idea as the Highway Code's advice to face oncoming traffic where there's no pavement.

How it's worked out

  • A route that's a main road plus closes off it becomes one continuous loop that ends back where you started, with no road walked twice on the same side.
  • Routes that go round a block need a few crossings to join the loops up. The route uses the fewest crossings it can, always at junctions, and each one is written into the directions.
  • The line on the map is drawn on the side of the road you're on, so the two sides of a road show as two parallel lines with arrows going opposite ways.

Where a route starts

  • Route's own start (default, for people going out on their own): drive routes start at their parking spot; others start at their busiest junction.
  • My location uses your phone's GPS, or tap the map to pretend you're somewhere. It lists the nearest routes in every ward by walking distance and starts the chosen one at the corner that's the shortest walk away. This is a first step towards generating routes on the spot around wherever someone is.
  • Event start (pub) is the action-day behaviour: every walk route sets off from the ward's pub.

Rural wards (Gnosall & Woodseaves)

  • These routes are built without a start point (each local area just uses its own centre) and sized by effort rather than homes. A home on a sparse lane counts as about six town homes, following Patriot's rule of thumb that 40 rural homes take as long as 250 town ones. That gives full-size walking routes in the villages and 20–110-home driving routes on the lanes.
  • On driving routes, lanes are delivered both sides in one pass ("drive along, delivering both sides", then "head back, already done"). Village streets on the same route are still walked one pavement at a time.
  • The sheet only records how many homes each road has, not where along it they are, so a long road with its houses bunched in a village looks sparser than it is.

Catching up your progress

  • Optional, on by default. Each time you open or come back to the page it takes one location reading, works out which step you're on from where you were last time and how long ago, and offers to tick off the steps in between. It never ticks anything without asking, and nothing is recorded while your phone is locked.
  • It ignores detours up driveways, and counts stretches you skipped by going in at one house and out further down. In simulated tests about 9 in 10 catch-ups land on the right step or the one next to it, and mistakes lean towards under-ticking.
  • To try it at a desk, tick "Desk test" and tap the map where you'd be each time you look at your phone.

Known gaps in this prototype

  • Walks to a route, and between separate parts of one, follow real roads: the page loads the live Stafford sheet for its walking network, including the no-homes roads listed below the ###ROUTE_PLANNER_ONLY_BELOW### marker. Any remaining gap under 120 m is bridged with a straight link; bigger ones fall back to a straight line.
  • We don't know which sides actually have houses or pavements, so homes are split 50/50 between the two sides.
  • The data is a frozen copy of the published Penkside, Littleworth and Baswich apps.
